A founder’s story explains why the work exists.

On the official donation page, Natasha Pein describes growing up in the Soviet Union, where state antizionism was embedded in schools, newspapers, and daily life, and how that experience shaped the decision to build STOPAZ with Dr. Naya Lekht.

The experience behind the mission

Soviet internal passports recorded nationality on the “Fifth Line,” making Jewish identity visible to the state.Pein describes professional and educational barriers that followed Jews without needing to be written openly into law.She recalls antizionism not as an abstract theory but as schoolyard hostility, exclusion, propaganda, and pressure to prove belonging.Her argument is that the vocabulary changes across eras while the mechanism of collective suspicion and exclusion can persist.That history became part of the case for education that helps people recognize antizionist ideas before they harden into policy and practice.Contributions support the research, curriculum, training, public education, events, leadership development, and advocacy through which STOPAZ carries out that work.
